2. Why PCOS

Unmet need. Polycystic ovary syndrome affects roughly 1 in 10 women of reproductive age (Bozdag et al., 2016). No drug is approved specifically for PCOS. Treatment is symptomatic and borrowed: metformin (diabetes), combined oral contraceptives, spironolactone (hypertension), letrozole and clomiphene (fertility) (Teede et al., 2023). Insulin resistance, hyperandrogenism and chronic low-grade inflammation are the three biological axes (Escobar-Morreale, 2018).

The gap. The borrowed drugs were found one at a time by clinical intuition. There is no systematic, evidence-weighted ranking of all approved drugs against the PCOS gene set. That ranking is the promised output.

Why repurposing. A new drug takes 10 to 15 years and costs over a billion dollars (DiMasi et al., 2016); an approved drug already has safety and pharmacokinetic data, so a repurposing lead can move to a trial in a fraction of the time (Pushpakom et al., 2018).

The honest risk of PCOS as a choice. The disease gene set is small: about 19 to 20 GWAS loci (Day et al., 2018) and a few hundred associations in Open Targets, many with low evidence. The big-data argument therefore cannot rest on the disease side. It rests on the drug side (all bioactivity data), the network (the full human interactome) and, optionally, expression signatures.

4. Why it is a big data problem, honestly

StepScaleLaptop?
Filter ChEMBL to approved drugs and join to targets20 M activity rows in, ~55 k rows outInput exceeds 8 GB RAM; needs a Spark join or a database. Output is small.
Build PCOS seed set from Open Targets + GWAS2 GB Parquet in, ~300 to 500 genes outSpark reads only the needed columns; result is small.
Network proximity for every approved drug on STRING2,800 drugs × 12 M edges, degree-matched random controls (1,000 per drug)No. ~2.8 million shortest-path computations. Embarrassingly parallel across drugs: Spark map over drug partitions with the graph broadcast.
Signature reversal against LINCS (optional)3 M profiles, 100+ GBNo. Chunked or distributed processing required.

The four Vs, with numbers. Volume: 20 M activities, 12 M edges, optionally 3 M expression profiles. Variety: bioactivity tables, evidence strings, a graph, expression matrices, trial records, each with its own identifiers. Veracity: predicted versus experimental interactions, evidence scores from 0 to 1, licence-restricted sources excluded. Velocity: ChEMBL and Open Targets release quarterly, so the pipeline must re-run, which is an argument for automation, not for streaming.

Why Spark rather than Hadoop MapReduce. Network propagation and proximity are iterative; Spark keeps intermediate state in memory (Zaharia et al., 2016), MapReduce writes to disk each iteration (Dean & Ghemawat, 2008). Open Targets already ships Parquet, Spark's native columnar format (Apache Software Foundation, n.d.).

5. Pipeline

ChEMBL + DrugBank
approved drugs → targets→ID mapping
UniProt ↔ Ensembl ↔ STRING→Drug target sets→Network proximity on STRING
Spark, per-drug map→Ranked candidates→Hold-out validation
known PCOS drugs
Open Targets + GWAS Catalog
PCOS seed genes with evidence scores→ feeds proximity step
  1. Ingest. Download ChEMBL (Parquet), DrugBank XML, Open Targets associations (Parquet), GWAS Catalog TSV, STRING human links. Store in HDFS or a local object store as Parquet. Tool: Spark.
  2. Approved drug set. ChEMBL molecules with max_phase = 4, cross-checked against DrugBank "approved". Output ~2,800 drugs.
  3. Drug target sets. ChEMBL activities with pchembl ≥ 6 (1 µM) on human single-protein targets, joined to the approved set. Spark join over 20 M rows. Output: drug → set of UniProt accessions.
  4. PCOS seed set. Open Targets associations for EFO_0000660 with overall score ≥ 0.1, plus GWAS Catalog mapped genes. Weight = evidence score. Output ~300 to 500 Ensembl genes.
  5. Identifier mapping. UniProt ↔ Ensembl ↔ STRING protein IDs. Log every unmapped ID; report the loss rate.
  6. Network proximity (Guney et al., 2016). For each drug: mean shortest-path distance from its targets to the seed set on STRING (score ≥ 400), compared with 1,000 degree-matched random target sets, giving a z-score. Spark map over drugs, graph as broadcast variable.
  7. Rank and annotate. Sort by z-score; attach for each drug its current indication, its closest PCOS genes and the evidence type behind them (the evidence path).
  8. Validate. Before step 6, hold out drugs already used or in trials for PCOS (metformin, spironolactone, letrozole, clomiphene, pioglitazone, liraglutide, statins, inositol where listed). Measure recall of the hold-out in the top 50 and AUROC against all other approved drugs, following the trial-based validation in Cheng et al., 2018.
  9. Optional second line of evidence. Signature reversal: PCOS granulosa or adipose signature from GEO versus LINCS drug signatures; report drugs that score on both lines.

7. Expected outcome and success measure

Deliverable. A ranked table of approved drugs with proximity z-score, current indication, closest PCOS genes and evidence type, plus a network figure for the top 10.

Success measure. At least 4 of the roughly 8 held-out drugs already used for PCOS appear in the top 50 of about 2,800, against under 0.2 expected by chance; AUROC above 0.7. If the hold-out is not recovered, the negative result still shows whether PCOS biology is reachable through the current target space, which is itself informative.

8. Challenges and limitations

  • Identifier mapping across five sources is the hardest engineering step; a 5 to 10% loss of targets is typical and must be reported.
  • Small, noisy disease gene set. Mitigation: weight seeds by evidence type and test whether genetic seeds alone recover the hold-out better than all seeds.
  • Literature bias. Well-studied proteins have more edges and more evidence, so proximity favours them. Mitigation: degree-matched randomisation, as in the chosen method.
  • Data leakage. If known PCOS drugs stay in the seed-building step, the validation is circular. Mitigation: hold-out before scoring.
  • Honest limitation. The output is a computational prioritisation; it needs experimental and clinical validation, and cell-line expression signatures, if used, do not represent ovarian tissue.

10. Writing checklist

  • Every claimed V has a size next to it.
  • The laptop-breaking step is named with a memory or compute reason.
  • Each source has size, format, licence and one example record.
  • The identifier-mapping step is written down.
  • Known PCOS drugs are held out, not reported as discoveries.
  • A success number and its chance baseline are stated.
  • A diagram is drawn.
  • Written in your own words; the reasoning must be yours to defend.
